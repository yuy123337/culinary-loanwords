The linguist Dan Jurafsky once found that the fancier the restaurant, the more foreign the words on its menu (The Language of Food, 2014). I've always been amazed by how much of the world a single dining experience can contain. In fine dining, this almost seems to be the norm. While reading English-language descriptions in the Michelin Guide, I noticed how many culinary terms remained untranslated. Sometimes, even within a single paragraph, several dishes retained their original names. I couldn't always understand them all, but that was part of what caught my attention.

When a dish keeps its name in another language, the name may do more than identify the food. Within an English-language description, it can become a small cultural cue, carrying associations with tradition, knowledge, and perhaps sophistication, beyond flavor itself.

Bourdieu captured something related in Distinction: “Taste classifies, and it classifies the classifier.” What we recognize as refined may also reflect the cultural knowledge we possess and the distinctions we make. I became curious whether borrowed culinary terms carry similar associations with prestige. Do certain terms appear in more luxurious contexts? And does that association correspond to the restaurant's actual price tier, or does it remain relatively stable across different settings?

To explore these questions, I extracted the contexts surrounding foreign culinary terms in Michelin Guide descriptions, used sentence embeddings to represent them, and examined their associations with predicted restaurant price tiers. This interactive visualization is one way of exploring those patterns.

There is, however, an important caveat. Everything here comes from the Michelin Guide's English-language descriptions, not restaurant menus or the cuisines themselves. The Guide offers a useful window into how an influential institution presents dining across countries, but it is also a selective and curated perspective.

What appears “foreign” or “fancy” is therefore being interpreted from an English-language vantage point. These words and cuisines come from different parts of the world, where they have their own histories, meanings, and often very ordinary everyday lives. We need to be careful not to mistake their associations with prestige in English-language descriptions for inherent qualities of the cultures themselves, or to reproduce an Anglocentric view that exoticizes cultural differences.

What this visualization captures is how culinary language is framed in a particular institutional context, rather than the value of the cuisines themselves.

And after exploring these patterns, I still find myself amazed by how much of the world can be brought together in a single dining experience, and how much even a short introduction can carry.

Yue Yin
